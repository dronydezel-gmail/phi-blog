App idea · feasibility exploration

An iPhone app that measures lux, blue light and melanopic light

Target: iPhone 17 Pro and later · 15 November 2026

Short version: this is buildable, but the camera has to do the measuring. The iPhone 17 Pro has two ambient light sensors (front and rear), and Apple doesn't let third-party apps read either one in real time.

With calibration, the camera can measure lux well. Blue light and melanopic lux can only be estimated from the camera's three colour channels. Those estimates hold up for normal white light and are unreliable for coloured or narrow-band light, so the app has to say when it's guessing.

For personal use, a small Bluetooth spectral sensor (section 07) gives much better melanopic readings and can log all day. Ready-made devices exist (section 08), but they cost far more and keep their data in their own apps, so building the sensor is the more practical route.

02Measuring lux with the camera

Read raw sensor data and divide out the exposure settings. What's left is proportional to how bright the light is:

S_c = (raw_c − black_c) / (t · ISO/100)      per channel R, G, B
E_v = k · (w_R·S_R + w_G·S_G + w_B·S_B)      k, w calibrated per iPhone model and camera

Here t is the shutter time, black_c is the sensor's black level and E_v is the lux reading. A rough version without raw data uses only the exposure settings: E ≈ C·N²/(t·ISO), where N is the aperture f-number and C is a calibration constant. It's less accurate because Apple's auto-exposure and tone mapping get in the way.

Design decisions

  • Use raw sensor data (Bayer RAW), not normal photos or video. Normal frames go through HDR and tone mapping, so pixel values stop being proportional to light. Raw captures through AVCapturePhotoOutput are linear, and 2–5 readings per second is enough for a meter.
    • On Pro models, Apple Log video uses a published curve that can be reversed, so it may work for a continuous mode.
    • Raw support on the front camera is limited, so check availableRawPhotoPixelFormatTypes for each camera.
  • Set exposure manually and adjust it automatically (setExposureModeCustom). Keep the brightest channel at roughly 20–60% of full scale. That should cover about 0.1 lx up to direct sun (~100,000 lx).
  • Account for the angle light arrives from. A lux reading should count light from the whole half-sphere above the sensor, weighted by angle. A lens only sees its field of view. Two fixes:
    1. A diffuser over the lens (a PTFE disc, a clip-on cap, even white paper). Most accurate, but it's an accessory.
    2. Correct for angle in software using the 120° ultra-wide. Use the lens calibration to work out each pixel's angle, weight the pixels accordingly and add them up. No accessory needed, but it misses light from beyond the frame edges: with even light from all directions, a frame reaching about 60° from centre captures about 75%. This would set the app apart.

Two measurement modes

  • Desk: phone flat, camera facing up. The standard lighting-design measurement.
  • Eye level: phone held at eye height, rear camera pointing where you look. This is how the melanopic standard (CIE S 026) and the sleep and circadian recommendations define the measurement.

03Blue light and melanopic lux

  • Melanopic EDI (melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance, from CIE S 026:2018) is lux weighted for the eye's melanopsin cells, which drive the body clock. The formula is melanopic irradiance (mW/m²) ÷ 1.3262, or equivalently lux × mDER, where mDER is a ratio that depends on the light's spectrum.
  • Why it can only be estimated: melanopsin is most sensitive around 490 nm, between the camera's blue and green channels. Three channels can't pin it down exactly. The app has to estimate it from what real light sources typically look like.

Three approaches, from simplest to best

  1. Colour temperature lookup. Estimate colour temperature from RGB, then use a typical mDER: about 0.4–0.5 for warm 2700–3000 K LEDs, about 0.6 at 4000 K, about 0.9–1.0 for daylight. These are approximate figures, so check them against the CIE S 026 toolbox before relying on them. Crude, but robust for white light.
  2. Fit a model to a library of real light spectra (daylight, incandescent, LED, fluorescent, screens). Map camera RGB to all five standard eye-response values, including melanopic. One study using RGB colour sensors got within ±3.3% of the values calculated from full spectra (Trinh et al., Sensors 2023). Those were dedicated sensors with known responses, so treat ±3.3% as a best case, not what a phone will do. A smartphone-specific version was published by Lucassen et al., CIC 2019.
  3. Flag low confidence. Measure how far the light's colour is from normal white light. If it's far off (RGB LED strips, coloured light), show "estimate unreliable".

Pick a specific "blue light" number

"Blue light" on its own is vague. Options:

  • S-cone-opic EDI: the blue-cone measure from the same standard. The camera's blue channel tracks it well, so it's the easiest to estimate.
  • Melanopic EDI: the body-clock measure, and the best headline number.
  • Skip the IEC 62471 blue-light hazard measure for v1. It measures retinal damage risk, and it needs a different kind of measurement.

Product hook

The 2022 expert recommendations (Brown et al., PLOS Biology) for melanopic EDI at eye level:

Daytime
≥ 250 lx
Last 3 h before bed
≤ 10 lx
While sleeping
≤ 1 lx

A red/amber/green gauge against these limits tells people what to do, not just a number.

04Extras specific to the iPhone 17 Pro

  • The 48 MP ultra-wide makes the software angle correction possible.
  • Flicker measurement. The camera reads its sensor row by row (rolling shutter). In a short-exposure frame of an evenly lit surface, brightness changes from row to row show the light flickering over time. That gives flicker percentage and frequency, which few apps offer.
  • Calibrate each camera separately: the main camera for dim light, the ultra-wide for wide coverage.
  • Keep the sensor input swappable in case Apple ever opens up the ambient light sensors.

06What's hard

  • Calibration is most of the work. Each iPhone model and camera needs:
    • an absolute scale, checked against a reference lux meter
    • a correction for darker image corners
    • the spectrum model, checked against a spectrometer (Sekonic C-800 or UPRtek MK350 class) across 30–100 real light sources
    The free tools luox.app and the CIE S 026 toolbox calculate the true reference values from measured spectra.
  • No background measuring. iOS won't let the camera run in the background, so the app can take spot readings or run a foreground "monitor" mode on a stand. It can't log all day like a wearable. The Bluetooth sensor in section 07 solves this.

Suggested tech: SwiftUI, AVFoundation (raw capture, manual exposure), Accelerate/Metal for processing raw frames, SwiftData for the log, Swift Charts, App Intents for a quick-measure shortcut.

Suggested plan

  1. Test build (a few days): raw capture plus the exposure maths, compared against any lux meter in about 10 scenes. This checks that readings scale correctly across dim to bright light.
  2. v0.1: calibrated lux and colour temperature, desk and eye-level modes, diffuser support.
  3. v0.2: melanopic and blue-light estimates, confidence flag, the bedtime/daytime gauge.
  4. v0.3: software angle correction, flicker, history. Optionally the Bluetooth spectral sensor from section 07 as a "pro" mode for accurate melanopic readings.

07Option: a Bluetooth spectral sensor

iOS doesn't let apps read the iPhone's own light sensors, not even personal builds installed from Xcode or TestFlight. For personal use, the most accurate option is a small external sensor that streams to the app over Bluetooth. It fixes the camera's two biggest limits: only three colour channels, and no background measuring.

Firmware

  • Start with Arduino (Seeed's nRF52 core with the Bluefruit library) for a quick prototype. Move to Zephyr / nRF Connect SDK later if battery life needs to improve.
  • Loop: wake every 10–30 s, adjust gain and integration time so counts sit at 10–80% of full scale, read all channels in one pass, save a record to flash, send it if the phone is connected, sleep.
  • Keep the firmware simple: send raw counts with the gain and integration time used. Do all the maths on the phone, so calibration can change without reflashing the device.
  • Storage: a ~50-byte record every 30 s is about 150 KB a day, so roughly two weeks fit in 2 MB of flash.
struct Sample {                 // ~50 bytes, little-endian
  uint32 seq;                   // increasing sample number, used for sync
  uint32 unixTime;              // seconds; clock set by the phone on connect
  uint8  gain;                  // sensor gain setting (0.5× … 2048×)
  uint16 atime, astep;          // integration time = (atime+1)·(astep+1)·2.78 µs
  uint16 counts[18];            // all channels from one auto-SMUX read
  uint8  flags;                 // saturated, 100/120 Hz flicker, battery low
}

iPhone app side

  • Background Bluetooth: CoreBluetooth with the bluetooth-central background mode and state restoration. iOS then relaunches the app when sensor data arrives, even after the system has closed it.
  • Gaps are covered. If you force-quit the app, iOS won't relaunch it. The device keeps buffering, and the app catches up from the last sample number when you open it.
  • Features this enables: daily light-dose charts, time spent above 250 lx melanopic EDI during the day, and an evening alert when it stays above 10 lx after a chosen time.

Calibration

  1. Without a spectrometer: build the matrix M from the sensor's published channel responses and the diffuser's transmission, using a large library of light spectra. Then set the absolute scale with a lux meter. This is quick, but it relies on the datasheet; differences between individual sensors and the housing will shift results.
  2. With a spectrometer: measure 30–100 real light sources with both instruments side by side and fit M directly. This is the version to trust.

Build steps

  1. Breadboard (an evening): sensor and board wired over I²C, printing raw counts over USB.
  2. Live Bluetooth: the live sample characteristic plus a minimal iOS screen showing lux and melanopic EDI from the datasheet-based matrix.
  3. Logging: flash buffer, log transfer, background mode and state restoration.
  4. Hardware: housing, PTFE diffuser, calibration against reference instruments.
  5. App features: daily dose charts and evening alerts.

Safety: it's worn on the body, so use a LiPo cell with a protection circuit and a housing that doesn't press on the battery.

09Open questions

  • Purpose (decided): personal use, installed through a paid Apple developer account (direct from Xcode or via TestFlight). App Store review isn't a constraint, so a DIY sensor and debug screens are fine.
  • Accessories: is a cheap diffuser clip acceptable, or must it work with the phone alone?
  • Reference instrument: can you borrow or rent an MK350S-class spectrometer for calibration? It's the biggest factor in whether melanopic readings can be trusted.
