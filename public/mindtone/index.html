App idea · research report

Sell the rhythm engine, not relief

Research into an idea for an iOS meditation app: a four-layer tone metronome with pink noise, nature soundscapes and binaural beats.

15 November 2026 · about 45 minutes to read · 173 sources linked inline

The brief

An iOS tone and metronome app for meditation. It plays up to four concurrent rhythms, each with its own tone, interval and volume, at intervals from 0.01 seconds to 20 minutes. The tones are gongs, bells and chimes; the backgrounds are pink noise, rain, wind in trees and alpine streams. Pink noise matters most, on the belief that it reduces tinnitus. Binaural beats in the alpha (8–12 Hz) and theta (4–8 Hz) ranges are a wished-for extra, on the belief that they reduce stress and improve sleep. Working name: Mindtone.

Verdict

Build it, but as a precise meditation instrument and not as a remedy. No iOS app found in this research offers the core of the brief: four independent rhythm layers, each with its own tone, interval and volume, on a single scale that runs from fractions of a second to tens of minutes.

The feature the product owner ranks first, pink noise for tinnitus, is the weakest leg on every count that matters: no systematic review or guideline finds that sound of any colour reduces tinnitus or beats placebo, a tinnitus claim would very likely make the app a medical device in the EU and UK and would describe a Class II “tinnitus masker” in the US, and steady noise is already given away by Apple and others. Binaural beats fare slightly better (small, poorly blinded studies show reduced anxiety), but “brainwave entrainment” is unproven, nothing supports alpha or theta beats for sleep, and a 2026 sleep-laboratory trial found that continuous pink noise at 50 dB cost about 19 minutes of REM sleep a night.

The app should therefore launch as a general wellness tool that names no medical condition, defaults to a sleep timer, and leads with the rhythm engine, nature sounds and slow-breathing pacing, which are the parts with real support. Technically the whole product fits in one audio render callback that counts frames, with the 0.01-second extreme handled as what the ear actually hears, a 100 Hz tone; synthesising the noise, the binaural tones and most bells also removes most of the sound-licensing problem. Commercially this is an indie-scale product: 15% commission, a free core with a one-time unlock, a publicly listed trader address in the EU, strict product liability for software from 9 December 2026, and a new name in place of the working title, Mindtone, because “Mindtone: Binaural Beats, Noise” already trades on the App Store in the same category.

01The health beliefs hold up as comfort, not as treatment

Pink noise soothes while it plays; nothing shows it reduces tinnitus

The belief that pink noise is “effective for reducing tinnitus” overstates the literature. The Cochrane review of sound therapy (8 randomised trials, 590 participants, low-quality evidence) found “no evidence to support the superiority of sound therapy for tinnitus over waiting list control, placebo or education/information with no device” (Cochrane). Clinical guidelines run from lukewarm to hostile. The US otolaryngology guideline says clinicians “may” recommend sound therapy but “should” recommend cognitive behavioural therapy, or CBT (ASHA evidence map); the European guideline grades sound therapy “No Recommendation” (ASHA evidence map); the UK’s NICE made it a research question only (NICE); and the German S3 guideline, the most recent one read in full, says “noise generators or noisers should not be recommended for chronic tinnitus” and “sound therapy should not be practiced”, while strongly recommending behavioural therapy (German S3 guideline). The most telling experiment is the Tinnitus Retraining Therapy Trial, which compared counselling plus real sound generators with counselling plus placebo generators that tapered to silence. About half of every group improved, and the investigators concluded that neither treatment was better than the other (ClinicalTrials.gov; University of Alabama). The counselling carried the benefit, so an app that offers sound alone delivers the least-evidenced part of tinnitus care.

Nor is pink special. The only randomised comparison of noise colours (35 older adults, all wearing hearing aids with built-in sound generators for three months) found white, pink, speech-shaped and high-tone noise “equally effective”, and it had no untreated control, so even the shared improvement cannot be credited to the noise (Mondelli et al. 2021). Preference data point away from pink: a trade review of sound-therapy studies reports rain as the most preferred sound, with “a very high degree of individual variation” (Hearing Review), and one retraining study found patients choosing red (brown) noise because it resembled a shower (International Tinnitus Journal). Preference does not predict benefit either: in a small crossover trial, liking a sound did not correlate with change in tinnitus (Durai & Searchfield 2017). The closest test of this app’s actual format is discouraging. When a University of Auckland team used a plain white-noise app as the control for a counselling-based app, press coverage says the white-noise group “reported no improvement” (BBC Science Focus).

The belief is still understandable, and the reason matters for design. Tinnitus UK describes sound therapy as safe and as a way to “alter your perception of your tinnitus or your reaction to it”: a pleasant low-level sound distracts while it plays and helps people fall asleep (Tinnitus UK). That supports relief during playback and a palette to choose from. It does not support a lasting reduction or a best colour. Clinical practice also sets the level lower than the word “masking” suggests: enrichment sound is kept “a little quieter than your tinnitus”, because loud masking “does nothing to encourage long-term habituation” and can make tinnitus seem louder once it stops, and bedside generators come with a timer that switches them off after sleep onset (Tinnitus UK). Two features that look like natural extensions should stay out. Tailor-made notched music, which removes a frequency band around the tinnitus pitch, showed no significant effect on tinnitus distress in two 2024 meta-analyses (BVS record; University of Naples record), and the German guideline says it “should not be practiced”, citing possible harm (German S3 guideline). The tinnitus apps that do have randomised-trial support, Oto and Kalmeda, are structured CBT programmes and not sound players (Flinders University; DOAJ).

Binaural beats: a small anxiety signal on an unproven mechanism

The second belief is half right at best. Reviews agree on one consistent finding, a reduction in state anxiety; a 2015 review called it the only application whose results were not “either solitary or contradictory” (Chaieb et al. 2015). The most-cited meta-analysis reports a medium effect of g = 0.45, though that figure pools memory, attention, anxiety and pain across 22 small studies and is not an anxiety-only estimate (Garcia-Argibay et al. 2019). The mechanism that marketing in this category relies on is not established. A 2023 systematic review of EEG studies counted 5 of 14 supporting brainwave entrainment and 8 contradicting it, with alpha-band entrainment supported in 2 of 5 studies and theta in 3 of 10, and concluded that this “casts serious doubts on whether the brainwave entrainment hypothesis is scientifically tenable” (Ingendoh et al. 2023). Against an active control, binaural beats entrained the cortex only weakly and did not change mood (Orozco Perez et al. 2020). That pattern suggests much of the anxiety effect is the general benefit of sitting still with calm audio, which is an inference from the studies and not a tested conclusion.

Sleep is the weakest part of the claim. No meta-analysis of binaural beats for sleep or insomnia was found. The few positive sleep studies are small, used healthy volunteers, and used delta-range beats of 0.25 to 3 Hz, not alpha or theta (Jirakittayakorn & Wongsawat 2018; Fan et al. 2024), and a 2026 review of trials in young adults found no binaural-beat sleep trial to include (Elnazer 2026). The solid sleep evidence belongs to ordinary music (Frontiers in Sleep). One finding argues for a restriction: a large home-use experiment found that listening to 5 Hz and 15 Hz beats during reasoning and memory tasks worsened scores (Klichowski et al. 2023), so the feature belongs to rest and not to focus or study.

The literature does fix sensible defaults. Beats are perceived only when the two tones sit below about 1,000 Hz (Chaieb et al. 2015); EEG studies used carrier tones of 100 to 900 Hz, most often 400 Hz, and the entrainment-positive ones used 200, 250 and 400 Hz (Ingendoh et al. 2023). Longer exposure produced larger effects, and listening before an activity worked better than listening only during it; masking the beat with white or pink noise made no difference to outcomes (Garcia-Argibay et al. 2019). The EEG studies that embedded beats in pink noise mostly showed no entrainment, so the app must not claim that its pink noise enhances the beat (Ingendoh et al. 2023). A binaural beat exists only when each ear receives a different tone. Monaural beats and isochronic tones (one tone pulsed on and off) are physical modulation, work on loudspeakers and evoke larger cortical responses, but their outcome literature is even thinner (Chaieb et al. 2015; Healthline). No peer-reviewed report of binaural beats provoking a seizure was found; the epilepsy warnings that are standard in this category are precautionary (DrWeil.com).

Continuous pink noise overnight now has a measured cost

The popular story that pink noise improves sleep and memory comes from a different technology: short bursts of pink noise timed to slow brain waves through live EEG, which a phone app cannot do. Even there the memory effect shrank across replications from d = 0.99 in 2013 to d = −0.39 by 2021 (Harlow et al. 2023). For continuous playback, a systematic review of 38 studies rated the evidence that noise improves sleep as “very low” quality and warned that it “may also harm sleep and hearing” (Riedy et al. 2021). Then, in February 2026, a University of Pennsylvania sleep-laboratory study of 25 healthy adults found that pink noise alone at 50 dB, “about the level of moderate rainfall”, reduced REM sleep by about 19 minutes a night, and that adding pink noise to aircraft noise shortened both deep and REM sleep, where earplugs largely protected sleep (Penn Medicine; SLEEP). The study was short, used young healthy sleepers and tested two levels, and only the press material was read for this report, so the dose-response is unknown. It did not include people with tinnitus, whose enrichment sound is normally set quieter, and no study has measured sleep stages under tinnitus-style overnight sound. The defensible position is that all-night broadband noise can no longer be presented as harmless.

Hearing dose is not the binding constraint at these levels. The WHO-ITU safe-listening standard gives adults a weekly allowance equivalent to 80 dBA for 40 hours, and iOS applies the same rule to headphone audio and logs it in the Health app without any work by the developer (WHO; Apple Support). Applying the standard’s equal-energy rule, eight hours a night for a week uses about 0.14% of the allowance at 50 dBA and 1.4% at 60 dBA, but about 14% at 70 dBA and 140% at 80 dBA. At quiet enrichment levels the risk is to sleep quality and not to the cochlea. At high headphone volume, an overnight session can trip the iOS Headphone Safety notification, after which the system lowers the volume at the next connection (Apple Support), and users will blame the app. The design consequences are a sleep timer with fade-out as the default, all-night playback as an explicit opt-in, a conservative default level, and no positioning for infants, for whom the Penn authors specifically caution against broadband noise.

The best-supported features are the ones the brief treats as secondary

A meta-analysis in PNAS (18 studies) associated natural sounds with lower stress and annoyance (Hedges’ g = −0.60) and found that water sounds had the largest effect on health and positive-affect outcomes, although most measures were self-reported (PNAS; Carleton University). Rain and alpine streams are therefore the easiest features to describe truthfully, and they match what tinnitus patients tend to choose. The metronome has an evidence-based use that the brief does not mention, which is pacing slow breathing. Heart-rate-variability biofeedback, which trains breathing at around six breaths a minute, is associated with large reductions in self-reported stress and anxiety (g ≈ 0.8 across 24 studies) (Goessl et al. 2017). A later analysis presented as conference slides puts the average resonance rate at about 11 seconds per breath, within a typical range of 4.5 to 6.5 breaths a minute, and finds paced breathing alone slightly weaker than full biofeedback (Lehrer & Brinkman slides). A 10 to 11 second cycle sits comfortably inside the app’s interval range. Interval bells themselves rest on tradition and not on trials: in the Plum Village practice, hearing a bell is the cue to stop and return to the breath (Thai Plum Village), and no controlled study of interval bells was found. That is a statement about practice, and the app can make it as one.

02Every ingredient is a commodity; the four-layer engine is not

The figures in this section come from the US App Store on 15 November 2026. Interval bells are a commodity: dozens of meditation timers offer them, several of the best-rated for free or for donations (Plum Village; Center). Almost all provide one repeating interval, or a list of one-off bells inside a session of fixed length. The nearest neighbours each cover one piece of the brief. Insight Timer allows several bells, each with its own bell type, repeating “anywhere from 5 seconds to 30 minutes apart”, but documents no per-bell volume and sits inside a content platform with 449,346 US ratings (Insight Timer help; App Store). MindTime gives every bell role its own level but has a single interval bell (App Store). Chime Squirrel can “run multiple chimes simultaneously”, yet a 2026 reviewer says it “does not work in the background” (App Store). The shortest interval documented in the timer category is 1 second (Meditate) and the longest repeating interval is Insight Timer’s 30 minutes.

Musicians’ metronomes have the multi-layer engine with the wrong sounds and the wrong range. Poly Metronome Pro runs “up to 5 polyrhythm voices, each with its own sound” (App Store), and a 2026 multi-track metronome from SheepiesLab gives every track its own tempo, instrument and volume for $3.99 (App Store). Every metronome found stops somewhere between 1 and 900 beats per minute (Metronome Beats), which is 60 seconds down to about 0.07 seconds between beats; Soundbrenner’s manual says its app cannot go below 20 beats per minute (Soundbrenner). Their voices are percussion clicks. The planned range of 0.01 seconds to 20 minutes therefore has a specific shape against the market: the 20-minute end is already served by timers, the sub-second end by metronomes down to 0.07 seconds, and nothing serves 0.01 seconds. What nobody offers is one continuous scale across both worlds, with independent layers and long-decay meditation tones.

Noise playback is the most saturated and price-pressured part of the plan. Since iOS 26 Apple ships 16 free Background Sounds with an equaliser, a stop timer and Shortcuts automation, and its support page includes a tinnitus section saying the sounds “can help minimize distractions” (MacRumors; Apple Support). TMSOFT gives away a real-time generator with a continuous brown-to-violet colour slider and “no audio files included” (App Store), and myNoise sells shapeable generators that never loop for a one-time $14.99 or $19.99 (App Store). Apple’s list has no item named pink noise, which leaves room for a precisely generated one, but playing steady noise in the background with a timer is no longer something anyone can charge for. Dedicated tinnitus apps are weak competitors: the largest, ReSound Tinnitus Relief, has 19,493 ratings and was last updated in May 2025 (App Store), against 279,250 ratings for White Noise Lite (App Store). That gap agrees with a 2019 survey in which 75% of people with tinnitus had never used an app for it, and those who had mostly named apps built for sleep or relaxation (JMIR mHealth). People with tinnitus are reached through general sound apps, which is where a wellness app that makes no claims already sits.

Binaural beats are equally available. TMSOFT’s free generator covers beat frequencies of 1 to 50 Hz (App Store), BrainWave has sold 40 programmes for a one-time $5.99 and holds 11,226 ratings (App Store), and subscription entrants such as Moongate (61,299 ratings since November 2023, $9.99 a month) show strong demand alongside a stream of billing complaints (App Store). A fixed alpha and theta generator has little value as a paid feature by itself.

No existing app combines the four pillars. The closest bundles are all 2026 releases with between zero and 13 ratings and, as far as their listings show, a single interval-bell layer. They validate the direction, and they also mean the simple version of the idea is no longer new.

Two clusters of complaints define the opening. The first is monetisation. Of the 100 most recent written US reviews, 71 for BetterSleep and 58 for Endel gave three stars or fewer, dominated by subscriptions replacing purchases, basics moved behind paywalls and trials that are hard to cancel (BetterSleep; Endel); a Samsara reviewer wrote “No way am I subscribing for a timer” (App Store). The second is unattended reliability. ReSound’s app “stops” when minimised (App Store), Mindfulness Bell “doesn’t ring” unless the phone is open (App Store), and a BrainWave user reports that “the audio cuts after a certain time, which wakes me up” (App Store). Written reviews skew negative, these samples are not representative, and Reddit could not be searched for this research. The complaints nonetheless point the same way: a one-time price with no account, and audio that survives a locked screen, a full night and a change of headphones.

Price precedent supports a one-time unlock of roughly $5 to $20: BrainWave at $5.99, metronomes at $2.99 to $9.99, myNoise at $14.99 to $19.99, and the new all-in-ones at $4.99 to $29.99. The audience is large but the money is not. Pooled tinnitus prevalence is 14.4% of adults worldwide (MedicalXpress), and an EU survey extrapolates to about 65 million adults with tinnitus, 26 million of them bothered by it (Biswas et al. 2022). Meanwhile the meditation-app category is estimated at about $2.2 billion for 2025 (Research and Markets) and is contracting at the top: Calm’s downloads fell 61% and Headspace’s 74% between 2018 and 2024 (Statista). The one indie sound app with revenue estimates, Dark Noise, appears to earn under $10,000 a month, on third-party figures that are unaudited (AppGoblin; Starter Story). Plan for that scale unless there is a budget for paid acquisition.

The working name, Mindtone, is a problem. “Mindtone: Binaural Beats, Noise” by PRODEKS UA, TOV is live on the App Store with 105 ratings at 4.5 stars, first released in April 2025 and last updated in August 2025, at $12.99 a month or $49.99 a year. It offers binaural beats, isochronic tones, soundscapes and a shut-off timer, and has no interval bells (App Store). Near-neighbours named MindTune and MindTime also exist (MindTune; MindTime). A second “Mindtone” in the same category would compete for the same search results and would invite a trademark dispute; no trademark search was done for this report, so one is needed before any name is fixed.

03One render callback carries the whole product

A single source node over a small C++ core

Every audio requirement in the brief can be rendered in one place. The recommended design is an audio session in the playback category feeding one AVAudioEngine with a single stereo AVAudioSourceNode, an Apple component available since iOS 13 that asks the app for audio one buffer at a time (Apple). The code inside that callback owns the noise generators, the two binaural oscillators, four rhythm schedulers with a preallocated pool of voices, and the readers for recorded loops. One callback means one clock, so the layers stay locked to each other to the sample, and the same continuous rendering is what keeps the app alive with the screen locked.

Apple’s rule for that callback is explicit: “Don’t allocate memory, perform file I/O, take locks, or interact with the Swift or Objective-C runtimes when rendering audio”, and Apple’s own reference sample writes its signal kernel in C++ “to ensure real-time safety” (Apple). Swift can break the rule silently through reference counting, closure captures and copy-on-write collections (Swift Forums). The practical split is a C++ core behind a plain C interface, a thin shim that hands the render block to the engine, and a Swift user interface. Simple values such as volumes and intervals cross to the audio thread as atomics (Swift’s Atomic type needs iOS 18 (Apple)), and larger changes such as loading a tone go through a preallocated lock-free queue. Under Swift 6 the render block must never capture an object bound to the main thread; that pattern has caused runtime crashes in other audio callbacks (Swift Forums). iOS 27 adds a render-block type that carries a real-time-safety guarantee (Apple), which can be adopted behind an availability check while iOS 26 remains supported. AudioKit would add a dependency over the same engine without solving the scheduling problem (AudioKit), and Apple’s old HelloMetronome sample is the pattern to avoid: it schedules beats from completion handlers and stores the beat length in a 32-bit float (Apple archive).

Count frames; timers fail at both ends of the range

The scheduler counts audio frames inside the render call. Each layer keeps the position of its next strike as a 64-bit floating-point frame number; on every call, any strike that falls inside the current buffer starts a voice at its exact offset, and the fractional remainder carries forward so the average rate is exact. Timers cannot do this at the short end. With the screen locked, the system enlarges the output buffer to 4,096 frames according to an archived Apple Q&A (Apple QA1606), about 85 milliseconds at 48 kHz, so eight or nine strikes at 100 per second fall inside a single buffer that is rendered in one go. Timers also fail at the long end: a developer who tried to start the engine from a background timer found that the start call failed, while audio that was already playing carried on (Apple Developer Forums).

Number types matter more than they look. At 48 kHz, 0.01 seconds is 480 frames and 20 minutes is 57.6 million frames. A 32-bit float represents whole numbers exactly only up to 16,777,216, which is 349.5 seconds of audio, so a float position silently loses precision after about six minutes; a signed 32-bit counter overflows after 12.4 hours. The core should use 64-bit integers for the running count and 64-bit floats for strike positions. Counted this way the layers never drift against each other. They drift against wall-clock time only by the tolerance of the output device’s clock, which matters only if a bell must land on the hour. When the user changes an interval mid-gap, the next strike is recomputed from the last one and clamped to the present, so shortening a 20-minute interval takes effect at once without a burst of catch-up strikes. When the output hardware’s sample rate or channel count changes, the engine stops and must be restarted by the app (Apple); running the core at a fixed 48 kHz and letting the engine’s mixer convert keeps the schedule and every filter coefficient constant, and that conversion behaviour should be confirmed on a device.

At 0.01 seconds the rhythm becomes a tone

A hundred strikes a second is not heard as rhythm. Periodic pulses below roughly 20 per second are heard as tapping and above roughly 50 as pitch, with the changeover somewhere in the 20 to 35 Hz region (PMC4264405; JARO 2022); these figures come from search summaries of several papers and are approximate. Played naively, the extreme is also ruinous: a gong sample with a 20-second tail retriggered every 10 milliseconds needs 2,000 overlapping voices per layer and 8,000 across four, and the summed level rises by more than 20 dB. Mathematically the steady state is simply a waveform repeating 100 times a second, so the listener hears a static 100 Hz buzz with the gong’s tonal colour and no strikes at all.

The handling follows standard sampler practice: a cap of 16 to 32 voices per layer, with the oldest voice faded out over a few milliseconds when the cap is reached (SFZ format); an envelope that shortens as the rate rises, so the full tail plays at a 20-minute interval and only a short grain at 10 milliseconds; and, above about 20 to 30 strikes a second, a tone mode that renders one period of the resulting waveform directly. Gain should fall as overlap rises, with headroom and a soft limiter on the output so four layers plus noise cannot clip, and the interface should show these rates in hertz because that is what the user hears. If the tones are synthesised as resonators (see the next section), re-striking a resonator at any rate costs the same processing, which makes the fast end nearly free. The product question is whether 0.01 seconds is a feature. It has one coherent use: between about 4 and 12 strikes a second, a pulsed tone is in effect an isochronic tone at theta and alpha rates, the loudspeaker-compatible relative of a binaural beat. That observation is this report’s inference, and it carries no stronger benefit claim than the beats themselves.

Pink noise and binaural tones are a few lines of arithmetic

For the app’s most important sound, Paul Kellett’s “refined” method is the best trade of quality against cost. It passes white noise through a weighted sum of simple filters using seven stored values per channel and is accurate to ±0.05 dB above 9.2 Hz at a 44.1 kHz sample rate; the cheaper Voss-McCartney family has ripple of roughly 0.5 to 2 dB (Robin Whittle’s pink-noise page), and Larry Trammell’s generator sits between them at about 0.25 dB (Trammell). No finite filter is exactly pink, but filtering can be made perceptually exact (Julius O. Smith). Each channel should run its own generator from its own random stream so the stereo image is wide and uncorrelated, and the noise should be generated live. The audible danger is a looped buffer a few seconds long, and a 32-bit random generator that is drawn once per sample repeats after about 25 hours, so a generator with 64 bits of state is the safer choice. A gentle high-pass filter near 10 to 20 Hz removes inaudible rumble that would otherwise consume headroom. Brown noise and a continuous tilt control come from the same machinery. Correctness is testable offline: render a minute, measure the spectrum in third-octave bands, and assert a slope of −3 dB per octave.

Binaural beats are two sine oscillators, one per ear, at the carrier frequency minus and plus half the beat frequency, written directly into the left and right buffers. Frequencies and gains must be smoothed sample by sample and the phase never reset; Apple’s sample ramps changes over 100 milliseconds because abrupt changes “can produce audible artifacts” (Apple). The two tones must bypass any stage that mixes the channels.

The output path, not the synthesis, is the binaural risk

An app cannot force true two-ear stereo. The Mono Audio accessibility setting, a single earbud, a mono Bluetooth speaker and the user’s Spatialize Stereo setting each turn a binaural beat into an ordinary acoustic beat. Spatialize Stereo is the subtle one: it renders left and right as virtual loudspeakers, so each ear receives both tones. Two questions on Apple’s developer forums asking how to detect or disable that setting remain unanswered (Apple Developer Forums; Apple Developer Forums). The only documented opt-out found is an Info.plist key that Apple describes for games, AVGameBypassSystemSpatialAudio, available from iOS 18 (Apple); whether it works for a plain stereo app is unverified and takes one line to test with AirPods Pro. The app can inspect the output route (Apple), but a Bluetooth route does not say whether it is a headphone or a speaker. The right behaviour is therefore a set of warnings and not a hard block: no headphone-type route, Mono Audio on, or spatial audio enabled each produce a plain-language hint, and a deliberate monaural-beat mode covers loudspeakers and single earbuds while being accurate about what the listener is getting.

Loops, background playback and the overnight case

Recorded ambiences reveal their seams in two ways: padding added by the encoder, and a discontinuity in the waveform at the loop point. Apple’s AAC encoder adds 2,112 priming frames, and only the CAF and M4A containers record how much to trim (Apple TN2258). Loops should ship with the crossfade already baked in, in CAF with AAC from Apple’s encoder or in a lossless format, and a unit test should confirm that each decoded loop has exactly the authored length. Memory sets the playback strategy. Decoded stereo audio at 48 kHz in 32-bit float costs 23 MB a minute, and an Apple developer-support engineer’s guide figure for an app that stays safe in the background is under 100 MB (Apple Developer Forums), so only short loops can live fully in memory and longer ones must be streamed from disk. Repetition is hidden by layering loops of unrelated lengths (47, 61 and 73 seconds realign only after about 58 hours), random start offsets and slow independent gain drift. Whether attentive meditators accept short layered loops or need several minutes of unique material per layer has no published answer and should be settled by listening tests.

Background playback requires the playback category and the audio background mode, with the session activated and the engine started in the foreground on a user tap (Apple). That is necessary, and an Apple engineer is clear that it is no guarantee: the mode “allows your app to remain awake while your audio session is active, which isn’t quite the same as guaranteeing it will not be suspended”. His list of reasons a white-noise app stops overnight includes an interruption the app never resumed from, memory pressure, and the app going silent for too long (Apple Developer Forums). That last item is the exposed case for this product: a rhythm-only session with 20-minute gaps renders digital silence between strikes. Reports suggest a running engine survives this, no documentation promises it, and the threshold for “too long” is unstated, so it needs overnight soak tests on real devices under both iOS versions. Adding inaudible noise just to look active is the wrong fix, since App Review rejects apps that declare the audio mode without audible content (Apple Developer Forums). With a noise or ambience bed playing, the question does not arise.

Lock-screen controls force a choice. An app appears in Now Playing only if its session is non-mixable and it registers at least one remote command (WWDC22); a mixable session lets a podcast play alongside but gives up those controls. The default should be non-mixable, with a “play alongside other apps” switch, and without the ducking option, which Apple says not to use “for more than a few seconds” (Apple). Interruptions need full handling in both generations of the API: iOS 27 introduces session life-cycle notifications whose resume recommendation no longer depends on the interrupting app signalling that it has finished, and iOS 26 still uses the older interruption notification (Apple). Playback should pause when headphones disconnect, as Apple asks (Apple). One system behaviour cuts directly against meditation: AirPods Sleep Detection, introduced with iOS 26, pauses playback when it judges the wearer asleep, and a meditation developer reports sessions stopping after 15 to 20 minutes of stillness with no reason delivered to the app (Apple Developer Forums). The app needs a visible paused state and a help note about that setting. The sleep timer itself should be a frame countdown inside the core that triggers a fade of 30 to 120 seconds and then stops the engine.

No published measurement of overnight battery cost for a synthesis engine exists. An Apple engineer said in June 2026 that synthesis generally costs more processor time than file playback and recommended profiling both (Apple Developer Forums). The processing here is light, and the app should not request a small buffer, since larger buffers mean fewer wake-ups. Battery drain is a recurring complaint in competitor reviews, so an eight-hour locked-screen measurement on wired headphones and AirPods belongs in the plan.

The design is unusually testable. The core can be called directly with no audio hardware, and the full engine can render offline in manual rendering mode (Apple). Replace each tone with a single-sample impulse and assert the exact sample position of every strike at 0.01 seconds, at a non-integer interval, at 1 second and at 20 minutes; assert identical output for every buffer size; and assert that 12 hours at 0.01 seconds produce exactly 4,320,000 strikes. On reach, the same code runs on iPad. An independent Apple Watch app is a poor first step because background audio there requires a Bluetooth route (Apple); a remote control with haptic bells is the sensible watch product. Android later means the same C++ core under Google’s Oboe library (Oboe FAQ), which is a reason to fix the core’s C interface now.

04Synthesise most sounds, record the rest, license almost nothing

Standard sound-effect licences do not cover this app. They are licences to synchronise sounds with other media, and the recurring test is whether the end user can hear the licensed sound by itself as the thing they paid for. A Sound Effect bars “any product that is primarily a sound product” (A Sound Effect); Soundly bars uses where its sounds “constitute a primary value” (Soundly); Zapsplat bars sounds that are the “main value” of an app (Zapsplat); Epic Stock Media’s standard licence excludes “relaxation or meditation apps” (Epic Stock Media); and Splice forbids sublicensing sounds in isolation “even if you modify the original sample” (Splice). Layering rain under a bell does not cure this, because the user can solo any layer. The free Sonniss and Pixabay collections are ambiguous on the same point (Sonniss; Pixabay). Instrument sample libraries are stricter still: Spitfire Audio forbids anything “that can be construed as” a sample library (Spitfire Audio), and Audiobro’s licence bars use “in isolation” without a separate written licence (Audiobro). A single struck bell note offered as a selectable interval tone is exactly the excluded case.

Four sources are clean: public-domain dedications under CC0, the developer’s own recordings, commissioned recordings with a written assignment of rights, and synthesis. CC0 “is not a license; it is a public domain dedication” (Creative Commons), but Freesound warns that uploaders may not own what they upload (Freesound), so every CC0 asset needs an evidence file with its address, uploader, licence and date. Attribution licences are usable in principle with a credits screen, yet the Creative Commons FAQ says licensees may not apply technological measures that restrict reuse (Creative Commons), and whether App Store copy protection breaches that has no authoritative answer; avoiding such material is cheaper than resolving the question. Three vendors sell an explicit licence for this kind of app (myNoise’s “embedded license”, Epic Stock Media’s Custom Application License and TunePocket’s Streaming License), all priced by enquiry (myNoise; TunePocket). Subscription licences carry a second hazard for a long-lived app, because the right to ship new versions can lapse when the subscription ends (Soundly).

Synthesis is the strongest answer for the tones. Bells, bowls and chimes behave as nearly linear resonators, so a bank of decaying sine-like modes at the right inharmonic ratios reproduces them; in the textbook formulation the model is “a parallel bank of second-order filters” (Julius O. Smith). Measured ratios are published: one Himalayan singing bowl sounds at 1 : 2.78 : 5.18 : 8.16 times its fundamental (Csound manual), a tubular chime at 1 : 2.76 : 5.41 : 8.93, and every bell mode is a slightly split pair that produces “very slow beats that are clearly audible” (Fletcher 1999). Permissively licensed code exists, notably the resonator from Mutable Instruments’ Rings module under the MIT licence (GitHub); the same repository’s AVR projects are under GPL 3.0 and must be avoided (GitHub), since Apple’s terms conflict with the GPL (Free Software Foundation). A synthesised tone is a few dozen numbers instead of megabytes, can be tuned so that four layers relate consonantly, can vary slightly on each strike, and handles fast intervals at fixed cost. The limits are specific. Tam-tams and Chinese opera gongs are strongly nonlinear, with pitch glides of “as much as 20 per cent” and a delayed shimmer (Fletcher 1999), so those should be sampled or left out. Decay times per mode were not found in any source and must be measured from the developer’s own recordings, and no listening test comparing synthesised and sampled meditation bells was found, so the result has to be judged by ear.

Recording is cheap for struck instruments and expensive for nature. Freelance sound designers charge a median of about $525 a day worldwide and about $420 for a mid-level freelancer in the EU (day-rate aggregator), which puts ten to twenty commissioned tones at very roughly $900 to $1,900; a 32-bit-float field recorder costs about $300 (price tracker). These are order-of-magnitude figures from rate aggregators. The real constraint is room noise, because a bowl decays for tens of seconds into silence. Rain, wind and streams cost more because of travel, weather and discarded takes, so licensing existing recordings directly from an individual recordist, in writing, for worldwide and perpetual use inside an app where listeners can play them alone, is usually the cheaper route. For ambiences a hybrid design fits best. Wind is the texture that synthesis handles most convincingly, as filtered noise with slow random gusts; rain and running water can be modelled from bubbles and drops (Audiokinetic) but tend to sound generic, and the market leaders do not synthesise nature from scratch: myNoise mixes recorded stems and Endel rearranges composed ones (myNoise; Endel).

Generative audio is usable with care. Stability AI’s community licence allows commercial use below $1 million in annual revenue and states “you own outputs” (Stability AI); ElevenLabs forbids distributing output “as part of any sound, sample, effects, or music library or other collection of sounds” (ElevenLabs), which describes this app’s sound menu; and the weights of Meta’s AudioGen are non-commercial (GitHub). The US Copyright Office’s position is that purely prompted output is not protected by copyright (IPWatchdog), so a competitor could lift a generated rain loop from the app bundle without infringing.

Delivery follows from these choices. Ambiences should ship as AAC at about 192 to 256 kbps and tones as lossless audio or synthesis; by straightforward arithmetic, eight five-minute ambiences and ten tones come to roughly 60 to 90 MB in AAC against about 450 MB uncompressed. A game-audio industry reference for mobile is −16 LUFS average loudness with a true-peak ceiling of −1 dB (Game Audio Network Guild), and assets should be normalised so that equal slider positions sound equally loud. Optional sound packs must use Apple-hosted Background Assets, because On-Demand Resources are deprecated as of iOS 27 and App Store Connect will refuse them from new apps starting in early 2027 (App Store Connect Help).

The palette itself should follow what contemplative traditions use: struck bowl bells such as the Japanese rin and keisu, small high bells, wooden signals such as the han and the mokugyo, chimes, and a gong for long intervals (Sōtō Zen). Accurate names are both more respectful and more distinctive than “bell 1, bell 2”. The “Tibetan singing bowl” label is contested, with one scholar describing such bowls as “neither Tibetan nor ritual in origin” (Wikipedia), so “Himalayan singing bowl” is the safer name. Instruments should match intervals: wood and clicks below a second, small bells from one to ten seconds, bowls and gongs beyond. Startle depends on an abrupt onset, on evidence that is mostly from animals (PMC7075047), which argues for soft-mallet variants of every tone.

05A wellness tool, sold once, by a named EU trader

Marketing words, not features, draw the medical-device line

In the EU the test is the manufacturer’s intended purpose, and the guidance defines that from “the label, in the instructions for use or in promotional or sales materials or statements”. Software intended for “treatment or alleviation of disease” is a medical device, while “wellness or fitness apps” are not (MDCG 2019-11 rev.1). The App Store listing is promotional material. The UK regulator says so explicitly, naming the “App store description and category”; it lists phrases such as “Help/help with…” and “Can benefit those who suffer from…” as signs of a medical claim, and states that “general disclaimers (for example ‘this product is not a medical device’) are not acceptable if medical claims are made or implied elsewhere” (MHRA). In the US, the FDA’s General Wellness guidance of 6 January 2026 says a software function that plays music to “soothe and relax” and “manage stress” is not a device, and treats relaxation, stress management and sleep management as wellness claims only when no disease or condition is referenced (FDA). Tinnitus is where that shelter ends: a “tinnitus masker” is a defined Class II device “intended to generate noise of sufficient intensity and bandwidth to mask ringing in the ears” (21 CFR 874.3400), the FDA has cleared software products in that class (K221168), and its guidance tells firms to consider whether the agency actively regulates products of the same type (FDA). These readings come from the guidance texts and have not been tested with a regulatory lawyer; no enforcement case against a noise or binaural-beat app was found in any of the three jurisdictions.

The market has already sorted itself along this line. The tinnitus apps that make therapy claims are CE-marked medical devices: Oto as Class I (Hearing Health & Technology Matters), Kalmeda as a prescription app in Germany (Healthcare IT News), and Tinnitracks (Sonormed). General sound apps avoid the word “treat”. Taking the device route is not a realistic alternative for this product. Consultancy estimates put self-certified Class I marking at roughly €8,000 to €26,000 and three to six months (MedDeviceGuide), but the cost is the smaller obstacle: the guidance requires that medical claims be “supported by appropriate level of clinical evidence” (MDCG 2019-11 rev.1), and the first section of this report shows that such evidence does not exist for passive sound. The science closes that route before the budget does.

Efficacy wording that stays on the wellness side still needs substantiation. The UK advertising regulator upheld a complaint against a game app’s claim of “greatly improving your memory and sleep” because it expected clinical-trial evidence (ASA), and Lumosity paid $2 million to settle US Federal Trade Commission charges over unsupported cognitive claims (FTC). The law is therefore stricter than accuracy. A sentence such as “may help you notice your tinnitus less while it plays” is consistent with the evidence, yet it names a condition next to a benefit, which is the pattern regulators read as a medical claim. The working rule is to describe sound, relaxation and routine, and never to pair a condition with a benefit verb. The examples below are drafts by the researchers and need professional review before use.

The discipline has to cover the app name, subtitle, keyword field, screenshots, website and replies to reviews, and user testimonials repeated in marketing count as the developer’s own claims (MHRA). No source showed how regulators treat a hidden search keyword such as “tinnitus” with no visible claim, which makes that the single most valuable question to put to a regulatory lawyer. Inside the app, a neutral safety note is appropriate and expected: Apple’s guideline 1.4.1 says apps “should remind users to check with a doctor” (App Review Guidelines), and competitors’ binaural apps carry notices about epilepsy, driving and listening volume.

App Review rewards the rhythm engine and punishes claims

Apple’s guideline 4.3(b) names “sound effects” and “simple timers” among saturated categories that are accepted only if they offer “a meaningfully different or improved experience” (App Review Guidelines). A bare noise player with a timer is exposed to that rule; the four-layer engine, the binaural generator and the mixer are the differentiation to spell out in the review notes, with a screen recording of bells sounding from the lock screen to justify the background audio mode. Subtitles must not “make unverifiable product claims”. Since 26 March 2026, any new app whose primary or secondary category is Health & Fitness or Medical and that is distributed in the EEA, UK or US must declare whether it is a regulated medical device; answering “No” is permitted and the status is shown on the product page (Apple). A “No” beside copy about tinnitus relief would be an inconsistency visible to Apple and to regulators. On payment, unlocking features requires in-app purchase, tips are allowed, and auto-renewing subscriptions “must provide ongoing value to the customer” (App Review Guidelines), which sits uneasily with a mostly static sound generator.

Commission is 15%; the model should be a one-time unlock

Membership of the developer programme costs $99 a year (Apple), and a new developer pays 15% under the Small Business Program up to $1 million in annual proceeds (Apple). Under the unified EU terms that took effect on 1 October 2026, Apple’s in-app purchase costs 26% as standard and 15% for Small Business Program members; the alternative payment routes save at most five points and add reporting and tax duties (Apple), which is not worth it at this scale. Two EU-specific consequences deserve attention before enrolment. Under the Digital Services Act, a developer who earns any revenue is a trader, and the trader’s address, phone number and email are displayed on the EU product page (PPC Land), so a business address or a company is the usual protection for a sole developer. On VAT, the EU’s top court held in October 2025 that an app store can be treated, for VAT purposes, as the seller toward consumers (PwC); the developer’s own invoicing position toward Apple depends on national rules and needs an accountant.

Subscription benchmarks argue against a subscription here. RevenueCat’s 2026 report puts the Health & Fitness median for download-to-paid conversion at 2.9% (RevenueCat), and across all categories freemium apps convert about 2.1% within 35 days against 10.7% for hard paywalls, with first-year retention down to 28% for annual plans and 8% for monthly (RevenueCat blog). When Dark Noise moved to subscriptions in 2023, forum commenters called it “the poster child for apps that should never see a subscription model” (MacRumors Forums), and the most damaging reviews in this category follow changes of business model that stranded earlier buyers. The fitting model is a free core with a few sounds and one or two layers, a one-time unlock in the range the comparables support (about $10 to $20), and a tip jar. It should be priced so that it never has to be reversed. A one-time price also avoids the appearance of charging a recurring fee to people seeking relief from a chronic condition.

Collect nothing, warn clearly, and design for older ears

Apple defines collecting data as transmitting it off the device, and data “processed only on device is not ‘collected’” (Apple). An offline app with no account and no analytics can therefore carry a “Data Not Collected” label, though it still needs a privacy policy and, on secondary accounts, a privacy manifest for basic APIs such as settings storage (Bitrise). A tinnitus diary would hold health data, a special category under GDPR (Legiscope), and Apple bars storing personal health information in iCloud (App Review Guidelines), so it should stay out. “No account, no tracking, works offline” is also a credible selling point in a category where the UK regulator has told consumers to check how such apps store and use their data (GOV.UK).

Liability is about to tighten. The revised EU Product Liability Directive treats software, including apps, as a product under strict liability and applies to products placed on the market after 9 December 2026; compensable harm includes “medically recognized damage to psychological health”, and exposure runs for ten years, or 25 for latent injury (Cleary Gottlieb; Gibson Dunn). A launch after that date falls under it in every member state that has transposed it. The plausible harm scenarios are narrow: hearing damage from loud prolonged playback, an accident while listening to beats at the wheel, a seizure-related claim, and delayed medical care because of a relief claim. Volume defaults, timers and warnings address the first three, and making no relief claim addresses the fourth. The research indicates that terms of use cannot remove strict liability to consumers, so a limited company and product-liability insurance are points to raise with a local lawyer and a broker; no data on premiums was found.

The audience skews older than a typical meditation app’s. Tinnitus prevalence rises from 9.7% at ages 18 to 44 to 23.6% at 65 and over (QxMD abstract) and overlaps with hearing loss, which makes large text, VoiceOver labels on every control, high contrast and low-pitched tone options practical requirements. One reviewer with high-frequency hearing loss could not hear a competitor’s default chimes (App Store). Apple now shows Accessibility Nutrition Labels on product pages (Apple Newsroom), which gives a small app a visible way to show that support. On discovery, the head search terms belong to apps with hundreds of thousands of ratings, so the realistic targets are narrower phrases such as “interval bell”, “meditation timer” and “mindfulness bell”, supported by keyword-linked custom product pages (MobileAction) and featuring nominations. Tinnitus communities prohibit self-promotion (Tinnitus Talk), so they are not a launch channel.

The key risks rank as follows. The largest is a claims error: one phrase in a subtitle, keyword or screenshot can turn a wellness app into an unregistered medical device in three jurisdictions, and the product owner’s personal conviction about pink noise makes that phrase tempting. The second is commoditisation: Apple can extend its free Background Sounds at any time, and several small developers shipped timer-plus-noise bundles in 2026, three of them with binaural beats, so the lead in multi-layer rhythm is real but not durable without execution quality. The third is unattended reliability, where the undocumented cases (silent gaps between strikes, AirPods Sleep Detection, interruptions that never signal their end on iOS 26) can only be found by soak testing, and where failure produces the one-star reviews that sink competitors. The fourth is the binaural output path, which the system can silently defeat. The fifth is sound provenance, where a single contaminated asset is a removal risk under Apple’s intellectual-property rules. The sixth is rejection under guideline 4.3(b) if the submission reads as another noise player. The last is economic: with indie-scale revenue as the realistic case, the project has to be justified by modest income and personal use, not by the size of the tinnitus population.

Several questions are the developer’s to decide. The name comes first, followed by whether 0.01 seconds is a feature or a curiosity to hide behind tone mode, whether tinnitus is mentioned anywhere at all (only after legal advice), which store category to choose given that Health & Fitness triggers the medical-device declaration, the deployment floor (iOS 26 with availability checks is assumed here), the unlock price, and whether to trade as an individual or a company given the public trader address and the liability regime.

Other questions need verification, because this research could not settle them. None of the competitor apps was tested hands-on, so whether Insight Timer can run several repeating bells concurrently with per-bell volume, and whether OPUS Loop, Liquid Sounds and Ember are single-layer, should be checked on a device before the gap is advertised. The full text of the 2026 SLEEP study should be read for its second sound level and any dose-response. On the engineering side, the open items are whether a silent engine survives 20-minute gaps overnight, whether the spatial-audio opt-out key works for a non-game app, whether the audio file reader trims AAC padding exactly for the shipped loops, how much battery an eight-hour session uses, and whether synthesised bowls pass a listening test against recordings. On the legal side, they are a trademark search for the chosen name, national transposition of the Product Liability Directive in the developer’s member state, whether the European Accessibility Act covers a standalone consumer app from a microenterprise, and the exact obligations for trader status and VAT. An evidence report on sound therapy by IQWiG, the German health-technology assessment institute, commissioned for the guideline update, was listed as due in 2026 and was not found (INAHTA); it could shift the most negative guideline position in either direction.

—Conclusion

The brief ranks its features in the reverse order of their strength. Evidence, law and competition were examined separately, and each puts the rhythm engine first and tinnitus relief last: the engine is unserved, defensible in App Review and the natural search angle, while the relief claim is unsupported, regulated and free elsewhere. That convergence is the main finding, and it is good news, because the constraints are cheap to live with. Making no medical claims costs nothing to build. Synthesising the sounds removes licence fees and shrinks the download. Collecting no data removes most privacy work. A one-time price removes the retention problem that defines subscription apps in this category.

The research also surfaced two uses the brief did not name, and both fall out of the engine at no extra cost: a pacer for slow breathing, which has some of the strongest stress evidence of anything the app can do, and pulsed tones at 4 to 12 a second, which give the fast end of the interval range a purpose on loudspeakers where binaural beats cannot work. None of this prevents the product owner from using the app for their own tinnitus exactly as intended, at a level just below the ringing and with a timer at night. The app simply cannot say that it helps, and the decision that determines everything else is whether the owner accepts building a tool whose most personal use goes unadvertised.
